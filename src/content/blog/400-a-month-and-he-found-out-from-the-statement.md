Grace has sent her mother £400 a month for six years, as a transfer with no name. The argument was never about the money. And once last winter's boiler is counted, the real number is £550, which neither of them had seen.

**Grace and Femi Okafor.** The households in these Shorts are illustrations built from typical UK figures, not real people, and every number below is worked on the household's sheet.

## What happened

Femi learned about it from a bank statement. On the fourth of every month, for six years, £400 had left the account in a transfer with no name attached, and it was going to Grace's mother. Grace and Femi Okafor have two teenagers and bring home £9,800 a month between them, so the sum itself was never going to sink them. When the argument finally arrived, it was not really about money at all. It was about the fact that nobody had said anything.

Grace had not exactly been hiding it. The payment was regular and unremarkable, the kind of line that scrolls past without a second look. But a transfer without a label is a gap in a shared picture, and once Femi noticed it, the silence around it mattered more than the figure. Their income could carry the support comfortably. What it could not carry was the feeling of having been left out.

When they sat down to talk it through properly, the number they started from was bigger than £400. Last winter the boiler had failed, and Grace had paid £1,800 to fix it, straight out of the joint account, in the space of a single afternoon. Add that to a year of monthly support and the true cost came to £6,600. Spread across the year, it worked out at £550 a month. Neither of them had ever seen that figure written down, and it was the honest one.

That was the turn. The assumption had been that the money was the sore point, when in fact the £400 had never been the problem; the secrecy had. A figure that sits in the plan with a clear name on it gives nobody anything to argue about. Only an amount one partner does not know about can start a fight, and this one had been quietly doing exactly that.

So the support became two visible lines instead of one invisible one. The first is a bill called Mum, £400, paid on the fourth, sitting in the household plan where both of them can see it. The second is an envelope called Family emergency that takes £150 each month, so that when the next boiler goes, the money is already set aside and the fix is a simple transfer rather than a row. For anyone supporting a relative, the approach the Okafors landed on is the same: take the regular amount, add the emergencies, spread the total over the year, and say the result out loud tonight, somewhere the other person can see it.
