## What happened

Marcus and Jen have never said the number £4,200 out loud. As far as they were concerned, the holiday cost £2,800. Marcus is a sales director paid a base salary plus commission, and Jen works part time, so their income arrives in good quarters and lean ones, and that pattern matters to how this went.

The £2,800 was the booking: the flights and the apartment. It was paid in a good quarter, in one go, and once it was paid the holiday felt done. Then the holiday itself happened. Spending money while they were away came to £900. The airport added £260, between the parking, the early breakfast and the things people buy simply because they are standing in an airport. The dog stayed at a sitter's for ten days, which cost £240.

That was £1,400 that had never counted as the holiday. It was a third of the real total, it went on the card, and it landed in a month with no commission. The booking had taken the good quarter, so the rest fell on the bad one that followed.

That is the turn. The booking is only two-thirds of a holiday. The other third arrives while everyone is wearing shorts and nobody is counting, and because it comes in small pieces rather than one payment, it never feels like part of the price. For a household on a base and commission, that third does particular damage, because it reliably lands on the quarter that was never meant to pay for it.

The change was to price the holiday whole. The booking, the spending money, the airport, the animals and the taxi home all go into one figure, which for Marcus and Jen is £4,200, and the holiday envelope is for that amount rather than the part with a receipt. When a good quarter comes in, it funds the whole trip, not just two-thirds of it, and the holiday stops quietly costing the quarter after it.

Anyone can find their own version of that third. Take the last holiday's booking, then add every line from the ten days around it: the spending, the travel to and from the airport, the pet care, the meals on the way. Divide those extra lines by the total, and that is the share that never made it into the plan. Marcus and Jen's envelope now has a single target, the whole £4,200, so the third after the booking lands on the envelope rather than on the card.
