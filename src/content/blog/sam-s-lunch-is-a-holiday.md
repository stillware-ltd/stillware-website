A meal deal and a coffee, four days a week, in the staff room: £6.20. Nobody budgets £6.20. Over forty-three working weeks it is £1,066, and their holiday is £1,100. Not a lecture about sandwiches. Just the number.

**Sam and Jo, teacher and nurse.** The households in these Shorts are illustrations built from typical UK figures, not real people, and every number below is worked on the household's sheet.

## What happened

Sam and Jo, a teacher and a nurse, take home £4,700 a month. Sam's lunch costs £1,066 a year. So, near enough, does their holiday. Neither of them had ever put those two numbers side by side.

The lunch itself is ordinary. A meal deal and a coffee, £6.20, bought four days a week and eaten in the staff room. Nobody budgets £6.20. It does not feel like a number at all; it feels like a habit, the same way the walk to work is a habit. Which is exactly why the useful question was not what it cost each day, but what it cost across the whole year.

The sum is short. Four days a week, over forty-three working weeks, at £6.20 a time, comes to £1,066. Their holiday, the one they put money aside for all year, costs £1,100. The lunch Sam barely noticed was costing almost exactly as much as the trip they spent twelve months saving towards.

The turn is not a lecture about sandwiches. Sam was free to keep buying lunch. The point was that he could now keep it knowing it was a holiday, not a coffee. A quiet leak is never the size of the purchase. It is the purchase, multiplied by all the times it was never counted. Seen at £6.20, the lunch was invisible. Seen at £1,066, it was a choice worth making on purpose, whichever way it went.

The method takes about two minutes. Take one weekday habit, multiply by the days a week, then by the working weeks in the year. Find the annual thing that number equals, something that already has a place in the household's plans. Then decide, with the real figure in front of you rather than the daily one.

Sam decided on a middle path. He kept two days of bought lunches and started packing the other two. That alone put about £533 a year back, roughly half the original figure, before he had changed anything else in the budget. His lunch spending now sits in its own category, showing every staff-room line and the running total for the month, so the habit stays a number he can see and decide about, rather than something that simply happens four times a week.

Six pounds twenty is not a number anyone budgets; one thousand and sixty-six is the holiday.
