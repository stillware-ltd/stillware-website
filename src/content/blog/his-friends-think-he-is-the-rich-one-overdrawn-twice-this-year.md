A friend at dinner says Dev must be saving loads. He says £41,000. Under the table his current account says £212, with six days to payday. Both numbers are true, and that is the problem.

**Dev, twenty-nine, software engineer.** The households in these Shorts are illustrations built from typical UK figures, not real people, and every number below is worked on the household's sheet.

## What happened

Among his friends, Dev is the one who is doing well. He works as a software engineer, and over dinner someone remarks that he must be saving a fortune. He tells them he is worth about £41,000, which is true. At the same moment, under the table, he is checking his current account, and it shows £212. That is also true. Only one of those figures, though, is any use when the bill for dinner arrives, and twice this year his bank has charged him for going overdrawn.

The two numbers describe completely different things. The £41,000 is held in company shares. It is real wealth, but it lives on paper, rising one week and falling the next, and none of it can be spent on an ordinary Tuesday. The £212 is what sits in his account late in the month, six days before payday. And it is not all his to spend, because £116 of bills are still due to leave before the next salary lands. Take those away and the honest figure for the rest of the week is £96.

That gap explains the overdraft charges. Both came in months when a bill went out a day earlier than expected and caught him short, and each one cost him £40. It is an odd position for the person everyone at the table considers rich. The trouble was that Dev had been answering the wrong question. Asking whether he is saving is not the same as asking whether he is fine this week, and a very good answer to the first had been concealing a poor answer to the second.

So he changed which number he looks at, and when. The second question, how much is safe to spend today once the bills already on their way are accounted for, now sits on his phone's home screen, where he sees it before he spends rather than afterwards. The shares still matter, but he checks them only once a month, on a fixed day, instead of letting their value colour how he feels about a Tuesday lunch.

When the subject comes up at dinner now, he gives two answers rather than one. He is worth more than he used to be, and he has £96 to last him until Friday. Both are accurate, and together they tell the full story. For anyone in a similar spot, the useful figure is the same one Dev landed on: what can safely be spent today after the bills already coming, kept somewhere it will be seen before the card comes out. Since he started doing that, there has not been another charge.
