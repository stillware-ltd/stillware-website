Eight thousand lands on Friday and the plans start on Thursday night. But the bad quarter before it left a £2,919 hole, and the card was "cleared when the commission lands". Eight thousand, minus the hole, minus the card: £1,281. A real number, and it funds the Quarter envelope.

**Marcus and Jen. He is a sales director on a base plus commission; she works part time..** The households in these Shorts are illustrations built from typical UK figures, not real people, and every number below is worked on the household's sheet.

## What happened

On Friday, £8,000 lands in Marcus and Jen's account. By the time the subtraction is done, it is worth £1,281. Marcus is a sales director on a base salary plus commission, and Jen works part time, so their income comes in uneven pieces: lean quarters, then a strong one that seems to fix everything.

This was the best quarter of the year. Eight thousand pounds in one payment, and the plans for it had started the night before it arrived. But the lump was not as large as it looked, because two things were already waiting for it.

The first was the hole left by the bad quarter before. That quarter had left them £2,919 short, and the gap had been covered from savings. The savings wanted it back. The second was the card. It held £3,800 that had been put there on the understanding that it would be cleared when the commission landed. Now the commission had landed.

Take £8,000, subtract the hole and subtract the card, and what remains is £1,281. That is the turn. A lump is never the size printed on the payslip. It is that size minus everything that was already waiting for it, and in a household paid by commission, something is always waiting. The plans that started on Thursday night were built on a number that never really existed.

The order was not a choice either. The hole came first, then the card, because both were promises already made. Only after those did the money become genuinely new, and the first pound left over was the first pound that was actually free to decide about.

And £1,281 is still a real sum with a real job. It goes into an envelope for the lean quarters, so that the next bad quarter does not dig the same hole again. If that works, the next £8,000 is worth all of it, because nothing will be waiting for it. That is the whole value of doing the subtraction: done properly, it only has to be done once.

Anyone expecting a lump, whether commission, a bonus or a refund, can do the same sum before it arrives. Write down everything that is already waiting for it, first. The size that remains is the size it really is. Marcus's commission now lands as unallocated money, the hole and the card are the first two moves, and what is left funds the envelope for the next lean quarter.
