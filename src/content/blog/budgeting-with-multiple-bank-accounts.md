**How many bank accounts do you need to budget? Fewer than the internet says — and the number that
matters is not how many accounts you open but how many transfers you then make.** The popular
six-account method costs **260 transfers a year** on a weekly payday, because your pay lands in one
of the six and you feed the other five every time. And six accounts still cannot keep this
household's **ten** committed bills apart, so four of them end up sharing a balance anyway — which
is the one thing separate accounts were supposed to prevent.

One thing worth saying before the arithmetic: almost everything ranking above this page on that
question is published by a company that would open the account for you — a lender, a fintech, a
savings platform. That does not make their advice wrong. It does mean nobody in that list has a
reason to tell you what running six accounts costs.

## The method is right about one thing, and it is the important thing

The usual advice is an account for each job: bills here, spending there, savings somewhere you do
not look. That is right, and the reason it works is **friction**. Money in another account is money
you have to decide to go and get, and deciding twice is the whole point. A number on a screen does
not stop anybody; a locked account sometimes does.

Keep that. The rest of this page is about what the other five accounts cost you and whether they buy
anything.

## What six accounts actually cost: 260 transfers a year

Nothing arrives in the right account. Your pay lands in **one** of them, and then you move it.

| accounts you keep | moves every payday | paydays a year | transfers a year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 (the usual advice) | 5 | 52 | **260** |
| 10 (one per envelope) | 9 | 52 | **468** |
| 1 (with named envelopes) | 0 | 52 | **0** |

Two hundred and sixty decisions a year, every one of them a decision you already made when you set
the system up. Put them on standing orders and you have saved yourself the Friday — you have not
saved yourself the second column. The money still moves, the balances still have to be reconciled,
and each account is still a set of terms, a card and a login.

Nobody opens ten. That is why the advice stops at six: not because six is a principle, but because
six is what a person can stand.

That is **ten** things with a price on them. Six accounts cannot hold ten numbers, so four of them
go in with somebody else — and those four are the ones you will guess about.

## Why a shared balance answers the wrong question

Put the five monthly bills in one "bills account" and it holds **£890**. On the day the water bill
leaves, the account still reads £890, and it cannot tell you whether the rent is safe. The balance
answers *how much is in here*. The question you actually have is *which of these is covered*.

That is not a failure of discipline. It is what happens when five claims share one number.

## A pot is a place; an envelope is a claim

UK banking has made this confusing, because Monzo pots and Starling spaces feel like the answer. A
**pot is a place**: the money moves into it and your main balance drops. An **envelope is not a
place** — it is a claim on the one balance you already have. Ten names point at parts of it and
nothing leaves the bank.

That distinction is the whole argument. If the money moves, you are still running transfers; you
have just moved them inside one app.

## The method, on paper, in four steps

You do not need software for this. You need somewhere to write ten numbers where you can see them
together.

1. **List everything with a price on it** — dated bills, this month's one-offs, and the everyday
   categories. For this household that is ten lines.
2. **Count your accounts.** If the first number is bigger than the second, some of them share, and
   you already know you are not going to open one each.
3. **Fund the list on payday**, once, on paper or on a screen. Nothing moves between accounts.
4. **Keep one locked account** if friction is what stops you spending. One is the useful number; the
   other five only organise what you can already see.

If any of those bills is already behind, that is a different problem and a better first call:
[StepChange](https://www.stepchange.org/) or Citizens Advice, free, before any budgeting method.

## Frequently asked

**How many bank accounts should I have in the UK?**
There is no legal limit — you can hold as many as banks will approve. Practically: one current
account for the money to land in, plus one you find hard to reach if friction is what stops you
spending. Adding accounts beyond that adds transfers, not clarity.

**Does the six-account method work?**
It works in the sense that it stops you spending committed money, and that is real. It costs 260
transfers a year on a weekly payday, and it cannot separate more claims than you have accounts.

**Are Monzo pots or Starling spaces the same as envelopes?**
No. A pot moves money out of your main balance; an envelope is a claim on a balance that stays put.
Pots are a real improvement on six separate banks, but you are still moving money.

**What if my bills are already behind?**
Ring [StepChange](https://www.stepchange.org/) or Citizens Advice first. Both are free, and neither
budgeting method fixes arrears.
