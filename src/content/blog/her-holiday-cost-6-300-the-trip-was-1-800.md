## What happened

The receipt said £1,800 for flights and a villa. That was the cheap part of Anita's holiday. Anita is thirty-nine, an IT contractor with her own company, and she pays herself £5,500 a month. She also bills by the day, at £450, and that detail changes what a fortnight away really costs.

Ten working days away from the desk is ten days with no invoice. At her day rate, that is £4,500 that never gets billed. Add the trip itself, and a fortnight in the sun comes to £6,300. The receipt showed less than a third of it.

Here is where it went wrong. Like most people, she budgeted for the trip. Nobody budgets for the silence that follows, the month when the invoice is half of what it normally is. So the month after the holiday, the money that pays her salary was short by £4,500, and it felt like a mystery. Nothing had gone wrong with the business and nothing unexpected had been bought. The account was simply lighter than it should have been.

It was not a mystery at all. It was the holiday, arriving on a different date from the holiday. That is the turn: for anyone paid by the day, time off is one of the biggest bills of the year, and it never shows up on a receipt. Because it has no receipt, it is easy to treat as free, right up until the invoice comes in short.

So her holiday envelope now has two lines rather than one. The first is the trip, at £1,800. The second is the days, at £4,500. Spread across the year, the full £6,300 works out at £525 a month. Saved that way, a week off becomes a decision she makes deliberately, rather than a wobble she discovers a month later when the company account is thinner than expected.

The lesson applies well beyond one contractor. Anyone who invoices by the day can price their time off the same way: count the days away, multiply by the rate, and add the trip. The receipt was never the cost. Anita's holiday envelope now takes £525 every month, the unbilled days included, so the short month after the next holiday is already saved for instead of arriving as a puzzle.
