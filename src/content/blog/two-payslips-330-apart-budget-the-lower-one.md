## What happened

Sam is a teacher and Jo is a nurse, and between them they take home £4,700 a month. On paper that is a steady household income. In practice, one half of it is anything but. Lay two of Jo's payslips side by side and the problem is plain: September paid £2,480, August paid £2,150. Asked which of those was her salary, there is no honest answer, because neither one is.

Nurses' pay moves with the rota. One month brings unsocial hours and the extra pay that comes with them; the next brings none. Jo's take-home swings by £330 from a good month to a lean one. When the couple built their plan, they did what most people would do with a figure that moves around: they took the average. For Jo, that came to £2,300, and the whole household budget was built on top of it.

The trouble is that £2,300 is a real number that has never once arrived in the account. In the low months, Jo's pay came in £150 under what the plan expected, and that gap quietly went on the credit card. In the high months, the extra simply got absorbed into ordinary spending. Neither event was dramatic enough for anyone to notice. The plan looked balanced on average and was short every time pay dipped, while the good months left nothing behind to show for themselves.

The turn was realising that, for anyone whose pay moves, the number to budget is the floor rather than the middle. Sam's £2,400 plus Jo's low month of £2,150 gives £4,550. That became the figure the whole plan was built on, and the order mattered: the floor first, before anything else. Budgeted on £4,550, every single month clears, including the lean ones, so nothing needs to go on the card to cover a shortfall.

Then came the high months. Instead of treating the extra £330 as income to be spent, Sam and Jo now treat it as a delivery with an address already written on it. The day it lands, it goes straight into the envelope for annual bills. It is exactly the same money they were earning before. What changed is that no month is short any more, and the good months have a job to do instead of vanishing into the weekly shop. The method itself is simple: look at the last six payslips, take the lowest one, and make that the budget, with an envelope waiting for everything that arrives above it.
