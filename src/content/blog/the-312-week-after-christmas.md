Priya knows about her eight monthly subscriptions. The three annual ones all bill in the first week of January, because that is when she signed up: £312 in one week, every year, every year a surprise. October is when January is cheap.

**Priya, thirty-four, product manager.** The households in these Shorts are illustrations built from typical UK figures, not real people, and every number below is worked on the household's sheet.

## What happened

It is the third of January, and £312 has left Priya's account in a single week. None of it was Christmas. Priya is thirty-four, a product manager taking home £4,600 a month, and this happens to her every year, and every year it arrives as a surprise.

The explanation sits in her subscriptions. She has eleven of them. Eight bill monthly, together costing £96 a month, and those she knows about because they show up all the time. The other three bill once a year, and all three land in January, because January is when she signed up for them. Cloud storage costs £96. A magazine costs £60. A piece of software costs £156. Together that is £312, falling in the month after Christmas, when money is already at its thinnest.

The turn is a change in what those three things are called. An annual subscription is not really a subscription at all. It is a bill with a date, and bills with dates belong on the calendar, not in a pile labelled subscriptions where they can be forgotten for eleven months at a time. Once they are seen as dated bills, the surprise disappears, because a date can be planned for.

So the decision moves from January back to October, when there is still time to make it calmly. Each of the three gets one of three choices. The first is to switch it to monthly and let it behave like an ordinary subscription. The second is to keep it annual and give it its own envelope: £26 a month covers all three, so the January bill is already saved by the time it arrives. The third is to cancel it, on the simple test of whether she would sign up today. Priya cancelled the magazine.

The order matters too. Start with the biggest date nearest to now, then deal with the next. October is the month when January is cheap, because there are still three monthly payments to spread it across rather than one week to absorb it. Priya's remaining annual renewals now have an envelope with a goal of £26 a month, and October's money is already in it. When the third of January comes round again, the week will be paid for a month at a time, and the only thing left of the old shock will be the line on the calendar.
